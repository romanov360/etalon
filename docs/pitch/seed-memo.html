The shift AI datacenters are replacing copper wires with light.
  • Copper stops working past a certain speed and distance. AI clusters passed that point.
  • So the wires become glass, and the signal becomes light.
  • Now the light is moving onto the chip package itself. The industry calls this co-packaged optics, or CPO.
  • Market for these parts: $4.2B in 2024 → $24.8B by 2030.
  • CPO is about 0.5% of AI-datacenter optics today, forecast at 30–35% of high-speed ports by 2030. The whole transition is ahead of us, not behind.
What does “talking in light” actually mean?

A copper wire is like shouting down a hallway. The faster you talk and the further you go, the more garbled it gets. Eventually you are spending more energy on the shouting than on the thinking.

A glass fiber is like a flashlight down that same hallway. Speed and distance mostly stop being the problem.

The hard part is the translator at each end: something that turns electricity into light and back again, small enough to sit next to the processor. That translator is a photonic chip — a silicon chip with channels for light etched into it instead of wires.

Every big AI chip is getting one. Almost nobody knows how to test them.

The break You cannot yet tell a good light chip from a bad one, cheaply.
  • Fully inspecting one photonic chip takes over 100 seconds. Electronic chips take milliseconds.
  • There is no agreed standard for that test. Much of the alignment is still done by hand.
  • One CPO package holds 24 or more optical engines.
  • One bad engine scraps the entire package. After co-packaging there is no rework.
  • TSMC names optical test, fiber attach, and packaging — not device physics — as the things blocking CPO.
Why is testing light so much harder than testing electronics?

To test an electronic chip you touch it with a metal needle. To test a photonic chip you have to shine light into it — and hit a target far smaller than the beam.

That is threading a needle, by hand, more than twenty times per package, on every package you build.

It is an egg carton where a single bad egg means you throw out the carton, except the carton is worth thousands of dollars and you only find out at the very end.

Why does who owns the software matter?

Because the buyers are now the same companies selling you the test machines.

If Teradyne owns your yield analytics, then Teradyne learns which of your chips fail and why — and sells that understanding, in aggregate, back into your industry. Chip companies have spent forty years refusing exactly this arrangement in electronics. They will refuse it here too.

The one remaining independent option, Luceda, is Chinese-owned. That disqualifies it from US defense floors and from a growing number of commercial ones.

So the customers have a real problem and no neutral answer. That is the opening.

What is a ring bank, and why can it be impossible?

To fit many channels down one fiber, each channel gets its own color of light. On the chip, each color is picked out by a microring — a tiny loop that resonates at one color, like a tuning fork that only answers to one note.

Fabrication leaves every ring slightly out of tune. So you put a small heater next to each one and warm it until it lands on its note. Standard practice.

The catch: heating one ring also warms its neighbors. The usual optimizer picks the cheapest arrangement while pretending the rings are thermally isolated from each other. They are not.

When we solved both problems together, some “cheapest” arrangements came back asking for negative heater power on two of eight rings — their neighbors' heat alone already pushes them past their target. A heater cannot make something colder. That bank can never lock, and the power number gets quietly better right up until it fails.

That is the class of problem this whole company exists to catch, and you only find it by building the whole thing.
